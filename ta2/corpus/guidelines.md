# Short health-guidelines document (general adult information)
Based on widely published public-health guidance (WHO, NHS). Group: verify each chunk against the cited source before the jury.

## What BMI is
Body Mass Index (BMI) is weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. It is a quick screening measure used to group adults by weight status. It is not a diagnosis and does not measure body fat directly. (Source: WHO.)

## Adult BMI categories
For adults, the WHO classification is: below 18.5 underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 normal weight, 25.0 to 29.9 overweight, and 30.0 or above obesity. (Source: WHO.)

## Limitation: muscle versus fat
BMI cannot tell muscle from fat. A muscular athlete can have a high BMI with low body fat, and a person with a normal BMI can still carry a high proportion of body fat. (Source: NHS.)

## Limitation: age, sex, ethnicity and fat distribution
BMI does not show where fat is stored, and the same BMI can mean different health risks at different ages, for each sex, and across ethnic groups. A WHO expert consultation noted that Asian populations can face higher health risk at lower BMI values than the standard cut-offs. (Source: WHO.)

## Physical activity for adults
Adults aged 18 to 64 should do at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days per week. (Source: WHO 2020 physical activity guidelines.)

## Healthy pace of weight change
A safe and sustainable rate of weight loss for most adults is about 0.5 to 1 kg per week, achieved through gradual changes in eating and activity. Very rapid weight loss and crash diets are not recommended. (Source: NHS.)

## Healthy eating basics
A healthy diet includes plenty of fruit, vegetables, legumes, nuts and whole grains. Free sugars should be below 10 percent of total energy intake and salt below 5 grams per day. (Source: WHO.)

## Being underweight
A BMI below 18.5 can have many causes, including not eating enough, illness or stress. A person who is underweight and unsure why should talk to a healthcare professional. (Source: NHS.)

## When to talk to a professional
BMI is a starting point only. Anyone worried about their weight, appetite, or health should speak to a doctor or registered dietitian, who can consider the whole person. Only a qualified professional can diagnose a health condition.

## Measuring height and weight accurately
For best results measure weight in light clothing without shoes, at the same time of day, and measure height standing straight against a wall without shoes. Record the same way each time so that changes over time are meaningful.

## Obesity classes
Obesity is further divided into three classes based on BMI: Class I (BMI 30.0 to 34.9) is moderate obesity, Class II (BMI 35.0 to 39.9) is severe obesity, and Class III (BMI 40.0 and above) is very severe or morbid obesity. Higher classes are associated with greater health risks. (Source: WHO.)

## BMI in children and adolescents
BMI in children and adolescents is interpreted differently from adults. It is plotted against age- and sex-specific growth charts, and the terms underweight, healthy weight, overweight, and obese are based on percentile cut-offs, not fixed numbers. This advisor is designed for adults only; parents with concerns about a child's weight should consult a paediatric healthcare professional. (Source: WHO/CDC.)

## Waist circumference as a complementary measure
Waist circumference measures abdominal fat and is a useful complement to BMI. The WHO recommends that waist circumference should be below 94 cm for men and below 80 cm for women to minimise metabolic health risk. Central (abdominal) obesity carries additional cardiovascular risk even at a normal BMI. (Source: WHO.)

## Healthy sleep and its link to weight
Poor sleep is associated with weight gain and obesity. Adults generally need 7 to 9 hours of sleep per night. Sleep deprivation can increase hunger hormones (ghrelin) and reduce satiety signals (leptin), making it harder to maintain a healthy weight. (Source: NHS / WHO.)

## Staying hydrated
Adequate hydration supports metabolism and can help with appetite regulation. Adults are generally advised to drink about 1.5 to 2 litres of water per day, though needs vary with body size, activity level, and climate. Sugary drinks add calories without nutritional benefit and are best limited. (Source: NHS.)

## Weight management during pregnancy
BMI calculations and weight-management goals change during pregnancy. Pregnant individuals should not aim to lose weight and should follow guidance from their midwife or doctor on healthy weight gain based on their pre-pregnancy BMI. This tool is not designed for use during pregnancy. (Source: NHS.)
